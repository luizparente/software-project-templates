## Overview
 
This section introduces your project proposal to stakeholders, board members, and potential investors. The introduction should orient readers to the general problem space and establish why your project matters in the broader context of the industry, market, or social landscape. You should briefly describe what your product is and why you believe it represents a good investment of time and resources.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 1–2 paragraphs that provide a high-level overview of your project. These paragraphs should answer the following questions: What domain or industry does your project address? Why does this domain matter? What is your product at a conceptual level, and why do you think it's a compelling idea? Avoid technical jargon in this section; write for a non-technical audience. Your tone should be professional but engaging, designed to capture reader interest and set the stage for the more detailed sections that follow.

## Executive Summary
 
The Executive Summary is a condensed, business-focused overview of your project. It is designed to be read quickly by busy executives and should clearly communicate the problem you are solving, the solution you propose, and why your solution is viable. This section typically follows the Introduction and precedes the detailed Project Overview.
 
### Problem Statement
 
Clearly articulate the specific problem that your project aims to solve. The problem statement should be concrete, measurable, and focused on a genuine pain point experienced by your target users or customers. It should explain why this problem matters and what the consequences are if it remains unsolved. Avoid vague or overly broad statements; instead, ground your problem in real user needs or market gaps.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 1 paragraph (approximately 150–250 words) that presents a single, well-defined problem. Begin by identifying your target users or stakeholders and describe the specific challenge they face. Use data, research findings, or user testimonials where possible to substantiate the problem's existence and significance. For example, if you are building a project management tool, you might quantify how much time teams waste on poor communication, cite studies showing the cost of project delays, or quote users describing their frustrations. Your problem statement should be specific enough that a skeptical reader could understand exactly what you are trying to fix and why it matters.
 
### Proposed Solution
 
Describe the core solution your product provides to address the problem statement. Explain *what* your product does, *how* it works (in conceptual terms), and *why* it is an effective solution to the identified problem. This section should demonstrate a clear logical link between the problem and your proposed remedy. Avoid implementation details (these come later); focus instead on the user-facing value and the mechanism by which your product solves the problem.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 1 paragraph (approximately 150–250 words) that clearly articulates your solution. Describe your product's primary features or capabilities and how they directly address the pain points you identified in the Problem Statement. Explain the reasoning behind your approach—why is this particular solution better than doing nothing, or better than obvious alternatives? Use concrete examples or scenarios to illustrate how users will interact with your product and what benefits they will realize. Your proposed solution should be sufficiently detailed to demonstrate feasibility, yet remain accessible to non-technical readers.

## Project Overview
 
The Project Overview section provides comprehensive, business-oriented details about your product, its market position, and its competitive landscape. This section is where you substantiate your claims with research, analysis, and benchmarking.
 
### Description
 
Provide a detailed description of your product, including its features, functionality, target users, and intended use cases. Go beyond the high-level summary in the Introduction and Proposed Solution; here you should paint a vivid picture of what your product is and how users will interact with it. Describe the primary and secondary features, the user experience, and the overall architecture or approach that makes your product distinctive.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 3–5 paragraphs (approximately 600–1000 words total) that comprehensively describe your product. Organize your description logically—for example, you might start with the core user experience, then describe primary features, then discuss secondary features or advanced capabilities. Use clear, specific language. If your product is complex, consider describing it from multiple angles (e.g., how different user personas interact with it, how it integrates with existing workflows, or how it scales). You may include user stories, workflows, or scenarios to illustrate typical interactions. Do not focus on technical implementation; instead, focus on what the product does and what the user experiences. By the end of this section, a reader should have a clear mental model of your product.

#### In Scope

Describe the scope of your project in detail. What is included? What are your goals? 

#### Out of Scope

Define what is excluded from your project, and why.
 
### Value Proposition
 
Explain the business value of your product. Why would customers choose to adopt your solution? What tangible benefits do they realize (cost savings, time savings, improved quality, new capabilities, risk mitigation, etc.)? Substantiate your value proposition with research, data, and citations. Reference industry reports, academic studies, market analyses, or user research that supports your claims. Your value proposition should be grounded in evidence, not speculation.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 3–5 paragraphs (approximately 600–1000 words) that justify your product's business value. Begin by identifying the key value drivers—the primary benefits that matter most to your target customers. For each value driver, provide supporting evidence: cite market research, academic studies, industry benchmarks, or user feedback. For example, if you claim your product will save customers 10 hours per week, cite the study or user research that supports this claim. If you claim there is strong market demand, reference market sizing reports or survey data. Include at least 5–10 citations in this section. Organize your paragraphs thematically (e.g., one paragraph on cost savings, another on productivity gains, another on risk reduction) so that each paragraph focuses on a single value dimension. By the end, a reader should understand not only *why* your product has value, but also *how much* value it creates, supported by evidence.
 
### Similar Products
 
Identify and analyze at least 5 similar or competing products in your market. For each product, explain: (1) what it does, (2) how it is similar to your product, (3) how it is different, and (4) its market position and business performance (market share, user base, revenue, reviews, strengths, and weaknesses). This analysis demonstrates that you understand your competitive landscape and that you have done thorough market research. It also sets up the Differentiators section by establishing a baseline against which to compare your product.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 3–5 paragraphs (approximately 600–1000 words) covering at least 5 similar products. For each product, spend roughly one-third of a paragraph (or more if space permits) describing it. Organize your analysis in a structured way—you might organize by competitor, or you might organize by product category or feature. For each product, explain its core value proposition, describe its target market, note its strengths and weaknesses, and cite metrics or evidence of its market performance (user reviews on app stores, Crunchbase data, press coverage, publicly available financial information, etc.). Be objective and factual; avoid hyperbole or dismissiveness. This section should demonstrate your expertise and show that you have done genuine competitive research. By the end, a reader should understand the competitive landscape and be prepared to understand how your product fits into it.
 
### Differentiators
 
Explain what makes your product different from and better than the competing products you identified in the Similar Products section. What is your competitive advantage? Identify at least one major differentiator—a significant feature, approach, business model, or positioning that sets your product apart in a meaningful way. Explain why this differentiator matters to customers and why it is difficult for competitors to replicate. Avoid claiming multiple trivial differences; instead, focus on substantial, defensible advantages.
 
**What we expect to see:** Write 3–5 paragraphs (approximately 600–1000 words) highlighting your key differentiators. Begin by identifying 1–3 major differentiators (focus on quality over quantity). For each differentiator, explain: (1) what it is specifically, (2) why it matters to customers, (3) how it compares to what competitors offer, and (4) why it is defensible (i.e., why competitors cannot easily copy it). Be specific and concrete. For example, instead of saying "our UI is better," explain precisely how your user interface solves a problem that competitors do not address, cite usability research that supports your claim, or describe a specific design innovation that is difficult to replicate. If your differentiator is based on a team capability, technical approach, business model, or partnership, explain why these factors create a sustainable advantage. Your differentiators should feel compelling and defensible when a reader finishes this section.

## References
 
List all sources cited in your proposal, using a consistent bibliographic format (APA, Chicago, or IEEE are common in academic and business contexts). Include at least 20 sources covering research, competitive analysis, market sizing, user research, and other evidence that substantiates your proposal. Your references should come from credible sources: academic journals, industry research firms (e.g., Gartner, IDC, Forrester), government data, established news outlets, official product documentation, and direct user research. Avoid relying solely on blog posts, unverified websites, or anecdotal claims.
 
**What we expect to see:** Compile at least 20 bibliographic entries for your proposal. Aim for a mix of source types: academic research (3–5 entries), industry reports and market research (3–5 entries), competitive and product analysis (5–8 entries), and other credible sources such as news articles, government reports, or case studies (3–5 entries). Ensure that every claim you make in the proposal—particularly in the Value Proposition, Similar Products, and Differentiators sections—is traceable to a source. Use a consistent citation format throughout. A strong reference section demonstrates rigor and builds credibility with your reader. A weak reference section (with few sources, non-credible sources, or sources that do not support your claims) will undermine otherwise compelling arguments.
